Most data problems in board decks are not accuracy problems. They are provenance problems: the number is plausible, but nobody can say where it came from or what it measures.

Use this checklist before any marketplace figure goes on a slide.

## 1. Source

Name the source, not just the platform. "Shopee" is not a source. A platform disclosure, a specific dataset, or a named research provider is.

## 2. Date

Two dates matter: the period the data covers and the date it was last refreshed. A figure covering Q3 that was refreshed in January is a different asset from a figure refreshed last week.

## 3. Definition

One sentence stating the metric: gross or net, orders or revenue, which market, which date basis. If the source does not define it, write "definition not stated" on the slide rather than guessing.

## 4. Estimation status

Reported, modelled or inferred. Say which. A modelled figure is usable, but only if the reader knows it is modelled.

## 5. Scope

Market, category and channel. Cross-border marketplaces make scope especially slippery, because seller location, buyer location and shipping destination are three different scopes.

## When a source fails the checklist

Do not silently upgrade a weak number into a strong claim. Two options work better:

- Present it as directional and say so in the sentence: "estimated, directional only".
- Drop it and use a metric you can defend instead. A deck with four defensible numbers beats a deck with twelve fragile ones.

## Keep the receipt

Store the source URL, access date and the raw figure next to the slide. When someone asks six months later, you will not be reconstructing it from memory.

Every dataset page on this site carries source, period, market and last-updated fields for exactly this reason.
